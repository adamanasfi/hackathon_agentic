The idea

Imagine a spinning wheel. If you take photos of it too slowly, it might look like it's spinning backward or slower than it actually is. The Shannon Sampling Theorem says that to perfectly capture the motion of the wheel (or any signal), you need to take photos (samples) at least twice as fast as the wheel's fastest spin. If you don't, you get an illusion called aliasing.

Why it matters

In digital audio, aliasing can make high-pitched sounds appear as lower, distorted frequencies. In digital video, it causes wagon wheels to appear to spin backward. In scientific measurements, undersampling can lead to misinterpreting data, making a fast-changing phenomenon look slow or even static. Understanding sampling is fundamental to correctly digitizing and processing any continuous signal.

The key equation

falias = |f - k · fs|
This equation describes the apparent frequency (falias) you observe when a signal of true frequency (f) is sampled at a rate (fs). The integer 'k' is chosen such that falias is minimized and falls within the range [0, fs/2].

    Grounding

    What comes from the paper, and what is ours

    Stated in the paper

    • "If a function f(t) contains no frequencies higher than W cps, it is completely determined by giving its ordinates at a series of points spaced 1/2W seconds apart." (Section II, Theorem 1)
    • This implies that the sampling rate must be at least 2W (where W is our 'f' for a single sinusoid) to perfectly reconstruct the signal.

    Our simplifications & examples

    • We focus on a single sinusoid for simplicity, rather than a general band-limited function.
    • We use 'f' for the signal frequency and 'fs' for the sampling frequency, consistent with modern notation, where Shannon used 'W' for the highest frequency component.
    • The phase (φ) and amplitude (A) controls are added to make the sinusoid more general, though the core aliasing concept depends only on f and fs.
    • The visual representation of the "reconstructed signal" as the lowest frequency sinusoid passing through the samples is an intuitive way to demonstrate aliasing, directly illustrating the ambiguity.

    Exploration 1: Meeting the Nyquist Criterion

    Try: Set the original signal frequency f to 1.0 Hz and the sampling frequency fs to 3.0 Hz. This means fs is greater than 2f (the Nyquist rate of 2.0 Hz).

    Watch: Observe the "Reconstructed Frequency (falias)" readout. It should be Hz, which is exactly the original frequency. The dashed reconstructed signal (purple) perfectly matches the original continuous signal (teal).

    Why: When the sampling rate is at least twice the signal's frequency, the samples contain enough information to perfectly reconstruct the original signal without any ambiguity.

    Exploration 2: Aliasing in Action

    Try: Keep f at 1.0 Hz, but reduce fs to 1.5 Hz. Now fs is less than 2f.

    Watch: The "Reconstructed Frequency (falias)" readout now shows Hz. The dashed reconstructed signal (purple) clearly has a lower frequency than the original signal (teal), even though it passes through all the sample points. The Nyquist Criterion readout should say "NOT MET".

    Why: With insufficient samples, multiple sinusoids can pass through the same sample points. The reconstruction algorithm picks the lowest frequency one, leading to the high-frequency signal being misinterpreted as a lower-frequency one.

    Limitation / common misunderstanding: Aliasing means signal loss

    A common misconception is that aliasing means the signal is completely lost or destroyed. While the original frequency information is indeed misinterpreted, the samples themselves are still valid points on the original signal. The problem is in the *interpretation* or *reconstruction* of the signal from these samples.

    Try: Set f to 1.0 Hz and fs to 1.0 Hz. This is an extreme case of undersampling.

    Watch: The "Reconstructed Frequency (falias)" will be Hz. The reconstructed signal (purple dashed line) is now a flat line (DC signal). This is because at this sampling rate, all samples happen to fall at the same point on each cycle of the original signal (e.g., all at the peak if phase is 0), making it appear as a constant value.

    Why: Even in this extreme case, the samples are correct values of the original signal. However, without sufficient information (i.e., enough samples per cycle), it's impossible to uniquely determine the original frequency, and the lowest frequency interpretation is a flat line.
