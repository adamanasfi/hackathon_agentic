The idea

Imagine you're trying to guess the temperature in a room. You have a thermometer (a measurement) and your own feeling (a prior belief). The Kalman filter is a smart way to combine these two pieces of information, especially when both are a bit uncertain, to get the best possible estimate. It's like finding the sweet spot between what you thought and what you just saw.

Why it matters

The Kalman filter is fundamental in many engineering applications, from guiding spacecraft and drones to tracking objects in computer vision and even predicting financial markets. It provides an optimal way to estimate the state of a dynamic system from noisy measurements. This specific excerpt focuses on the core "measurement update" step, where a new observation refines an existing estimate.

The key equation

K = P⁻ / (P⁻ + R)
x̂ = x̂⁻ + K (z − x̂⁻)
P = (1 − K) P⁻
These equations compute the Kalman Gain K, the posterior mean x̂ (updated estimate), and the posterior variance P (updated uncertainty) from a prior estimate and a new measurement.

    Grounding

    What comes from the paper, and what is ours

    Stated in the paper

    • "For a scalar state with prior estimate x̂⁻ and prior variance P⁻, and a measurement z with noise variance R, the optimal (minimum-variance) linear update is: K = P⁻ / (P⁻ + R); x̂ = x̂⁻ + K (z − x̂⁻); P = (1 − K) P⁻." (Section: Optimal estimate update, scalar form)
    • "The gain K weighs the measurement residual according to the relative uncertainty of the prediction and the measurement." (Section: Optimal estimate update, scalar form)

    Our simplifications & examples

    • We focus exclusively on the scalar measurement update step, omitting the prediction step and the multi-dimensional (matrix) forms of the equations.
    • We assume Gaussian noise for both the prior and measurement likelihood to visually represent them as bell curves, which is a standard assumption in Kalman filtering for optimal linear estimation.
    • The specific numerical ranges for sliders (e.g., -5 to 5 for means, 0.01 to 5 for variances) are chosen for clear visualization and to demonstrate edge cases.
    • The "bell curve" visualization for Gaussian distributions is an analogy to help build intuition for mean and variance as location and spread.

    Try: Set the Measurement Noise Variance (R) to a very small value, e.g., 0.01. Keep Prior Variance (P⁻) at 1.

    Watch: The Kalman Gain K will approach . The posterior mean x̂ will be very close to the measurement z (), and the posterior variance P will be very small (). The posterior Gaussian curve will be narrow and centered on the measurement.

    Why: When the measurement is highly certain (low R), the Kalman filter gives it almost all the weight (K ≈ 1), effectively ignoring the prior estimate and trusting the measurement completely. The uncertainty of the combined estimate becomes very low.

    Exploration 2: Trusting the Prior

    Try: Set the Prior Variance (P⁻) to a very small value, e.g., 0.01. Keep Measurement Noise Variance (R) at 1.

    Watch: The Kalman Gain K will approach . The posterior mean x̂ will be very close to the prior mean x̂⁻ (), and the posterior variance P will be very close to the prior variance P⁻ (). The posterior Gaussian curve will be narrow and centered on the prior mean.

    Why: When the prior estimate is highly certain (low P⁻), the Kalman filter gives it almost all the weight (K ≈ 0), effectively ignoring the new measurement and trusting the prior estimate. The uncertainty of the combined estimate remains close to the prior uncertainty.

    Limitation / common misunderstanding

    A common misconception is that combining information might sometimes increase uncertainty. However, the Kalman filter's optimal update guarantees that the posterior variance (P) is always less than or equal to both the prior variance (P⁻) and the measurement noise variance (R). You can observe this in the "Updating the Variance" slide: the posterior variance bar is always the shortest. This means that combining two pieces of information, even if they disagree, always leads to a more certain estimate (or at least not less certain).
