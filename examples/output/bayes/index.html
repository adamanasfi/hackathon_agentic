The idea

Imagine you're trying to figure out if you have a rare disease after getting a positive test result. Bayes' Rule helps you combine your initial suspicion (how common the disease is) with the test's accuracy to get a more realistic probability. It's like updating your detective's notebook with new evidence.

This lesson focuses on how the prevalence of a disease, the test's sensitivity, and its false positive rate interact to determine the actual probability of having the disease after a positive test.

Why it matters

In medicine, Bayes' Rule is crucial for interpreting diagnostic test results, especially for rare conditions. It helps avoid the "base-rate fallacy," where people overestimate the probability of disease after a positive test, ignoring how rare the disease truly is. Beyond medicine, Bayes' Rule is fundamental in machine learning (e.g., Naive Bayes classifiers), spam filtering, and any field requiring probabilistic inference and belief updating.

The key equation

P(D|+) = P(+|D)P(D)P(+)
where P(+) = P(+|D)P(D) + P(+|not D)P(not D)
This equation computes the posterior probability of having the disease (D) given a positive test result (+).

    Grounding

    What comes from the paper, and what is ours

    Stated in the paper

    • "The probability that two subsequent events will both happen is a ratio compounded of the probability of the 1st, and the probability of the 2nd on supposition the 1st happens." (Proposition 3, modern: P(A and B) = P(A)P(B|A))
    • "If there be two subsequent events, the probability of the 2nd b/N and the probability of both together P/N, and it being first discovered that the 2nd event has happened, from hence I guess that the 1st event has also happened, the probability I am in the right is P/b." (Proposition 5, modern: P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B))
    • The modern statement of Bayes' Rule P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B) is derived from these propositions.

    Our simplifications & examples

    • We use a diagnostic test scenario (Disease D, Positive test +) to illustrate Bayes' Rule, which is a common and intuitive application.
    • We use a fixed population size (e.g., 1000 individuals) and waffle charts to visually represent probabilities as counts, making the concepts more concrete.
    • The terms "sensitivity" and "false positive rate" are modern epidemiological terms used to describe P(+|D) and P(+|not D) respectively.
    • The "base-rate fallacy" is a cognitive bias that Bayes' Rule helps to correct, and we demonstrate it with a specific example.

    Limitation / common misunderstanding

    A common misconception is that a highly accurate test (e.g., 99% sensitive and 95% specific, meaning 5% false positive rate) guarantees a high probability of disease if you test positive. As shown in Exploration 2, this is not true for rare conditions.

    The base-rate fallacy is the tendency to ignore the prior probability (base rate) of an event when assessing its likelihood. In diagnostic testing, this means overlooking the disease's prevalence and focusing solely on test accuracy, leading to an overestimation of P(D|+).
